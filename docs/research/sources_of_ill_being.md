## 1. What drives ill-being, according to the book (focus: ages 7–22)

| Driver | What the book describes |
|---|---|
| **The digital environment** | Recommendation algorithms optimise for attention through the brain's reward system and push fear and anger, which spread faster than facts; endless short-video loops; comparison with filtered bodies and lives; cyberbullying, where most young victims do not tell an adult; early exposure to pornography. The book proposes a "media food pyramid" as a content diet. [book] |
| **Pressure** | Performance expectations at school (girls do better on average but carry more pressure); uncertainty about the future; harassment and violence; climate anxiety. [book] |
| **Family strain** | Parents are rushed, worried about money and their own uncertain futures; children are left alone with their problems. [book] |
| **Loneliness** | Isolation, linked to both the digital environment and family strain. [book] |
| **Inequality** | Better-off families buy private help early; others wait. The book warns this splits young people in two. [book] |
| **The system itself** | Fragmented, siloed services where nobody owns the whole picture; long waits despite the therapy guarantee; ping-pong between school, primary care and specialist care; staff shortages. [book] |

**Gendered pattern [book]:** girls tend to turn pressure inward (anxiety, depression); boys tend to turn it outward (behaviour problems, ADHD diagnoses, dropping out of school). The result is "two crises" that look different on the surface.

**Help-seeking [book]:** only about one in five young people who need help seek it themselves (book cites THL). The book reports the MIELI crisis line answered roughly a quarter of call attempts in 2024. *Our own earlier figure from MIELI's 2025 statistics is 35% answered (see `patient_paths`); both say the same thing: many calls go unanswered.*

### Figures from the book — verify before any slide

All approximate, all **[book]**, none checked:

- Cost of mental ill-health in Finland **about €11 bn a year, about 5% of GDP** (OECD and STM, as cited); roughly **doubled in ten years** (about €5 bn in 2013).
- About **1.5 million** mental-health visits by 7–22-year-olds in public primary and specialist care in 2023, by about 175,000 young people (THL, as cited).
- **Over 40%** of disability pensions are due to mental disorders (the book's context is young adults; check the exact scope).
- Direct costs of child and youth mental health care are a **small slice** of a roughly €30 bn health and social care budget.
- Authors' extrapolation (not a forecast): if trends continue, **by 2035 more than half** of girls and young women would live with anxiety or depression.

## 2. The book's radical solution: "Mieli 360"

A **single national youth mental-health service**, run in a distributed way and digital first [book]:

- **One door.** Anonymous entry, no diagnosis or referral needed.
- **AI pre-assessment in minutes**, then routing to the right person (nurse, psychologist, doctor), with humans making the decisions.
- **Data travels with the person**, so nobody has to retell their story.
- **24/7, where young people already are** (chat and social channels).
- **A low-threshold unit in every wellbeing county** and 5–6 regional expert hubs; links to schools; parents can follow progress.
- Models it builds on: **headspace** (Australia) and **Sekasin-chat** (Finland).
- Business case: not cheaper because it is cheap, but because care comes **earlier, right-sized and better targeted**, avoiding specialist and inpatient costs.

**The book's own recovery story** (a 17-year-old, "Ville"): a **daily check-in in an app** that keeps routines alive, **home digital neurostimulation** with **results visible to his doctor**, peer support, school adjustments, and shared data so he does not have to repeat his story. **That is Hiillos's loop**, with home stimulation as a partner (Sooma, Flow).

## 3. What this means for Hiillos [inference]

1. **Hiillos is the follow-up loop of a one-door system.** Mieli 360 opens the door; Hiillos keeps people from falling back out after treatment. Our vision line already says this: *nobody falls out of care just because they went home.* The "Where are you right now?" step (4 paths, path A = first-timers) is the bridge to the wider system.
2. **Causes leave traces Hiillos can see.** Hiillos cannot fix society, but it notices the footprint in a person's day: later screen-off, more time at home, fewer places, less contact. It answers with one small counter-step. The signal only asks; it never judges or reads content.
3. **No moralising about phones.** A wind-down step ("phone out of the bedroom tonight") is offered only when bedtime drifts against the person's own normal, and "no thanks" is always fine.
4. **Drift looks different for different people.** Withdrawal for some, restlessness and irritability for others. The clinic-set early signs (spec S3) already handle this; **do not hard-code gender rules.**
5. **Life context changes the right small step.** Someone with money worries or caring for a parent needs different steps. Later: links to community support (MIELI, Sekasin-chat, local services) as "actions" — roadmap only.
6. **Business case slide: the cost of doing nothing**, only with numbers checked at the primary source (THL, OECD, STM). The buyer is still the wellbeing county (see beyond-the-app §6).
7. **Young people are the scale-up path, not the demo.** The TMS cohort is adult; ask the mentors whether youth fits their work.
8. **Plug-in partners from the book:** Sekasin-chat (Sekasin Kollektiivi), MIELI, home neurostimulation (Sooma, Flow).

**Don'ts:** don't present the book's numbers as evidence; don't promise to reduce social-media harm; never track what people watch or write.
