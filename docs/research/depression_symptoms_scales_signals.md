# Score symptoms first, let sensors prompt

**Bottom line:** For a post-TMS home product, the measurement that has real evidence behind it is **a validated self-report scale on a fixed schedule with pre-agreed action thresholds**, not a sensor. In a randomised trial, scheduled self-report with algorithmic decision points roughly **doubled remission (73.8% vs 28.8%)** compared with usual care ([Guo et al. 2015](https://ajp.psychiatryonline.org/doi/10.1176/appi.ajp.2015.14050652)). The PHQ-9 is the best backbone: it is free, it maps onto the nine DSM-5 symptoms, a Finnish version is in use at THL, and **≥10 is the best cut-off** in a 17,357-person meta-analysis ([Levis et al. 2019](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/181475/1/bmj.l1476.full.pdf)). Its item 9 (thoughts of death or self-harm) is not optional to handle: any positive answer predicts a **roughly 10-fold higher one-year rate of suicide attempt and death**, with excess risk starting **within days** ([Simon et al. 2013](https://psychiatryonline.org/doi/abs/10.1176/appi.ps.201200587)). Passive signals are useful but weaker. Step count and rest-activity rhythm have the best evidence; heart-rate variability is a reliable but small group-level effect that antidepressants distort; voice and EEG separate patient groups in small studies but **no validated EEG biomarker for diagnosing or monitoring depression exists** ([Dev et al. 2022](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:ab8878c1-b868-4a22-8481-c2d3f3163d11/)). Almost all sensor evidence compares groups of people, while a relapse product needs to detect change *within one person*. The product should therefore learn a personal baseline after TMS discharge and use sensor deviations **to prompt an extra check-in, never to diagnose or to alert a clinician on their own**. For the weekend, two open datasets can be downloaded immediately (Depresjon and StudentLife); the others need credentialing or licence requests that take longer than a weekend.

## A four-layer stack: scales decide, sensors only prompt

This section is the practical recommendation. **Everything in the two tables below is the team's design inference, built from the evidence in the later sections. It is not established clinical practice and has not been tested in post-TMS patients.** It needs review by the clinical partner (the Aalto TMS group or HUS) before anyone treats it as a protocol.

The logic is simple. The companion report found that about half of TMS responders lose response by 6 months, that relapse risk is highest in the first 3–6 months, and that **84% of patients with early worsening regained benefit when TMS was reintroduced** ([Senova et al. 2019](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:0f08a658-0949-4837-8cdf-a788794d9fd0/); [Janicak et al. 2010](https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/durability-of-clinical-benefit-with-transcranial-magnetic-stimula/)). Catching worsening early has value. But the largest Nordic smartphone-monitoring trial (MONARCA II) found that **monitoring alone did not improve symptoms** ([MONARCA II](https://research.regionh.dk/en/publications/the-effect-of-smartphone-based-monitoring-on-illness-activity-in-/)), and the Guo trial shows why: the benefit of measurement-based care comes from **predefined actions at thresholds**, not from the charts. So every measurement in the stack is tied to an action.

A fortnightly PHQ-9 plus daily micro check-ins is the lower-burden option and is what this report recommends.

Users must also be told clearly who sees their answers and whether anyone is watching 24/7. A product that alerts clinicians about suicidality is likely to be a regulated medical device under EU MDR; this is an inference, and the regulatory analysis has not been done.

## Nine DSM symptoms, plus hopelessness and anxiety, define what to track

### The diagnostic criteria

DSM-5 defines a major depressive episode as **at least 5 of 9 symptoms in the same 2-week period**, as a change from earlier functioning, with at least one being depressed mood or loss of interest or pleasure ([MDCalc, DSM-5 criteria](https://www.mdcalc.com/calc/10195/dsm-5-criteria-major-depressive-disorder)) [verified]. The nine are: depressed mood most of the day nearly every day; markedly reduced interest or pleasure; significant weight or appetite change; insomnia or hypersomnia; psychomotor agitation or slowing that others can see; fatigue or loss of energy; worthlessness or excessive guilt; poor concentration or indecisiveness; and recurrent thoughts of death, suicidal ideation, a plan or an attempt. The symptoms must cause distress or impairment and must not be due to a substance, a medical condition, a psychotic disorder or a history of mania. Note that this criteria list was read on a clinical calculator site, not in the copyrighted APA text.

**ICD-11** uses the same 2-week core (depressed mood or reduced interest) and adds **hopelessness** to the accompanying symptoms. Code 6A70 is a single episode and 6A71 is recurrent. Importantly for this product, ICD-11 has subcodes for **"currently in partial remission" (6A70.6) and "currently in full remission" (6A70.7)** ([findacode ICD-11 6A70](https://www.findacode.com/icd-11/code-578635574.html)) [verified]. These give the clinician view a ready-made vocabulary. The ICD-11 cluster rule (at least 5 of 10 symptoms, one from the affective cluster) is [UNVERIFIED].

**Finland still codes depression with ICD-10** (F32 single episode, F33 recurrent). The Finnish Current Care guideline, as cited in a 2026 lecture, requires **at least 2 core symptoms and at least 4 of 10 symptoms in total, for 2 weeks or more** ([Isometsä lecture](https://asiakas.kotisivukone.com/files/gpfinland.kotisivukone.com/tiedostot/Kevatkoulutus_2026/Isometsa_Masennus_ja_itsemurhavaara_120526.pdf)) [secondary; from the companion report]. The detailed ICD-10 structure is [UNVERIFIED]: three "typical" symptoms (low mood, loss of interest, **reduced energy**), seven others (including an explicit "bleak, pessimistic view of the future"), and severity set by symptom count. Two differences matter for a Finnish product: ICD-10 treats **fatigue as a core symptom**, which DSM-5 does not, and both ICD-10 and ICD-11 include **hopelessness/pessimism**, which neither the PHQ-9 nor the QIDS-SR16 asks about directly. A daily check-in item on hope or future outlook would close that gap.

### Subtypes point sensors in opposite directions

The DSM-5-TR "specifiers" are [UNVERIFIED] in detail, because the page read did not list them, but their design meaning is important. **Melancholic** depression typically shows early-morning waking, worse mood in the morning, weight loss and visible psychomotor change. **Atypical** depression shows the opposite on two key sensors: **more sleep and more appetite**. A **seasonal** pattern puts the risk window in autumn and winter, which is relevant in Finland. The **anxious-distress** specifier captures tension and restlessness. The consequence is that **"sleep change" is not a one-direction signal.** A rule such as "less sleep means relapse" will miss half the people. The per-person baseline must learn *which way* each user moves, and the discharge interview should ask about it.

### Early-warning signs repeat within the same person

A 2021 systematic review of 25 longitudinal studies found a **distinct prodromal phase** before depressive episodes, commonly with **anxiety, tension, irritability and physical complaints**. The prodrome lasted from under a month to several years. Most useful for product design: **each person's prodrome was consistent across their own episodes**, and prodromal symptoms closely resembled the residual symptoms left over from the previous episode ([prodrome review, Psychother Psychosom 2021](https://www.sciencegate.app/document/10.1159/000517953); [Univ. Bologna record](https://cris.unibo.it/handle/11585/837668)) [verified abstract; author names not shown on the page read]. This is the strongest scientific argument for a **personal relapse signature** rather than a universal symptom list. It also means a depression-only scale can miss the first signs, because anxiety and irritability come early. That supports adding GAD-7 or a single anxiety/irritability check-in item.

Residual symptoms after treatment are an established relapse risk factor. A commonly cited older study (Paykel et al. 1995) reported relapse of **about 76% vs 25%** over 10–15 months in patients who remitted with versus without residual symptoms [UNVERIFIED — check before use]. The companion report has a verified, more recent figure: **each extra point of residual symptoms raised relapse hazard by about 7% (HR 1.07)** in an individual-patient meta-analysis ([Breedvelt et al. 2021](https://ore.exeter.ac.uk/repository/handle/10871/125915)). The commonly reported residual symptoms (sleep problems, low energy, poor concentration, anxiety, reduced interest) are [UNVERIFIED] as a ranked list, and **no source verified that any single symptom, such as sleep, reliably comes back first.** A small home-EEG paper repeats the general point that more than 80% of patients with depression have sleep complaints and that sleep worsening can be an early sign ([Kawamura et al. 2025](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:b97432c6-52bb-4bf5-aa87-949bcb8790a8/)) [abstract via Consensus; background statement, not a study result].

### Response, remission, relapse and recurrence have time rules

The terms come from the 1991 MacArthur task force, which found that researchers used them inconsistently and proposed a single scheme ([Frank et al. 1991, PubMed mirror](https://api-pubmed-ncbi.laibokeji.com/1929776)) [verified abstract; its numeric thresholds are UNVERIFIED]. The 2006 ACNP Task Force refined them ([Rush et al. 2006, QxMD record](https://www.qxmd.com/r/16794566)) [verified abstract]:

| Term | Standard meaning | Time rule | Commonly used score threshold | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Response** | Large improvement but not necessarily well | — | ≥ 50% drop from baseline score | Verified as used in [Guo 2015](https://ajp.psychiatryonline.org/doi/10.1176/appi.ajp.2015.14050652). ACNP calls response a weaker endpoint because it depends on baseline severity [verified abstract] |
| **Remission** | Minimal symptoms | **3 consecutive weeks** | HAM-D ≤ 7 [verified, Guo]; QIDS-SR16 ≤ 5 [verified, Guo]; MADRS ≤ 10 (sometimes ≤ 12) [UNVERIFIED]; PHQ-9 < 5 [UNVERIFIED] | Time rule verified (ACNP) |
| **Relapse** | The *same* episode returns, during remission and before recovery | Within the first 4 months after remission | Full episode criteria met again | Verified (ACNP) |
| **Recovery** | The episode is over | **≥ 4 months** of remission without relapse | — | Verified (ACNP) |
| **Recurrence** | A *new* episode after recovery | After recovery | Full episode criteria met again | Verified (ACNP) |

THL's Finnish PHQ-9 material adds a practical rule set: **"recovery" = start ≥ 10, end < 10 with a drop of ≥ 5 points; "reliable improvement" = a drop of ≥ 5 points while still ≥ 10** ([THL/Innokylä, Linnaranta](https://innokyla.fi/sites/default/files/2023-06/Linnaranta_PHQ-9%20saavutettava.pdf)) [verified]. Note that THL's word "recovery" here means something different from the ACNP "recovery". The clinician view should use one vocabulary consistently.

These rules have a direct consequence for the clinician screen. Many patients end a TMS course as partial responders (the companion report gives response of about 31–35% and remission of about 17–25% in the UK BRIGhTMIND trial). Under the ACNP rules, **a patient who never had 3 weeks of minimal symptoms is not "in remission", so worsening is continued non-remission, not relapse.** Worsening within 4 months of remission is relapse; after 4 months, it is recurrence. The clinician view should state which label applies, because the clinical response may differ. A systematic review of the empirical basis for these definitions exists (de Zwart et al. 2019, [Cambridge PDF](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/7FC54F95B5FC63FAD8D6709E873C1058/S2045796018000227a.pdf/empirical-evidence-for-definitions-of-episode-remission-recovery-relapse-and-recurrence-in-depression-a-systematic-review.pdf)), but its conclusions were **not verified**. **No guideline was found that defines relapse with PHQ-9 scores**; the PHQ-9 relapse rule in the stack is an inference.

## A fortnightly PHQ-9 is the backbone with trial evidence

### The PHQ-9 is free, Finnish and well validated

The PHQ-9 has nine items, one per DSM-5 symptom. Each is scored 0–3 ("not at all" to "nearly every day") for the **past 2 weeks**, giving a total of 0–27 ([THL/Innokylä](https://innokyla.fi/sites/default/files/2023-06/Linnaranta_PHQ-9%20saavutettava.pdf)) [verified]. The original validation in 6,000 primary-care and obstetrics patients found that a score of **≥ 10 had 88% sensitivity and 88% specificity** for major depression, and set the bands **5, 10, 15 and 20 = mild, moderate, moderately severe and severe** ([Kroenke et al. 2001, record](https://www.infontd.org/bibcite/export/ris/bibcite_reference/32168)) [verified abstract]. The DEPRESSD individual-participant meta-analysis (58 studies, n = 17,357, of whom 2,312 had major depression) confirmed that **≥ 10 maximised combined sensitivity and specificity** (0.88 and 0.85 against semi-structured interviews; 0.70 and 0.84 against fully structured ones). It also warned that **about half of positive screens in primary care are false positives** ([Levis et al. 2019](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/181475/1/bmj.l1476.full.pdf)) [verified]. For this product, that last point matters: the PHQ-9 is a screening and tracking tool. A clinician interview confirms a relapse, as the THL slides also stress.

The **Finnish version exists and is presented by THL**. Its stem reads "Kuinka usein viimeisen kahden viikon aikana ovat seuraavanlaiset ongelmat vaivanneet sinua?" and item 9 reads "Ajatuksia että olisi parempi jos olisit kuollut tai että haluaisit vahingoittaa itseäsi jotenkin." THL recommends it from age 14 ([THL/Innokylä](https://innokyla.fi/sites/default/files/2023-06/Linnaranta_PHQ-9%20saavutettava.pdf)) [verified]. A formal Finnish validation paper was not found. The PHQ-9 is also what HUS uses at rTMS referral (companion report), so home scores would be on the same scale as the referral. A Swedish version (needed for Finland's second official language) is believed to be available from the PHQ distribution site [UNVERIFIED].

### Scheduled scoring with action rules doubled remission in one trial

The best evidence for structured self-report comes from an RCT in Beijing (120 outpatients, 24 weeks of paroxetine or mirtazapine). The measurement-based-care arm scored QIDS-SR16 and a side-effect scale at **weeks 4, 6, 8, 10 and 12** and followed algorithmic dose rules. Compared with standard care, response was **86.9% vs 62.7%**, remission **73.8% vs 28.8%**, and time to remission **10.2 vs 19.2 weeks**, with no extra side-effect burden ([Guo et al. 2015](https://ajp.psychiatryonline.org/doi/10.1176/appi.ajp.2015.14050652); [Hofstra record](https://academicworks.medicine.hofstra.edu/publications/835)) [verified]. Two caveats: this is a single trial, and it tested acute medication treatment, not relapse prevention after TMS. The claim that measurement-based care is used by fewer than 20% of clinicians (Lewis et al. 2019) is [UNVERIFIED]. The common practice of giving the PHQ-9 at every contact, or every 2–4 weeks in the acute phase, is also [UNVERIFIED]. **No verified guideline gives a monitoring frequency for the continuation or maintenance phase.** The fortnightly cadence in the stack is therefore an inference from the PHQ-9 recall window and the Guo spacing.

### Daily check-ins work for a week; months are unproven

A meta-analysis of 68 mobile EMA datasets found **pooled compliance of 81.9% (95% CI 79.1–84.4)**, with no significant difference between clinical and non-clinical samples. But the typical study was short and intense: a **median of 5 prompts a day for 7 days** (12 days in clinical samples) ([Williams et al. 2021](https://doaj.org/article/59bc5eb53157483b8a28cf0a2a8f9e60)) [verified abstract]. A relapse product runs for months, so that design does not transfer. **No verified data were found on EMA adherence over several months in depression, or on how adherence changes during a relapse.** The companion report adds that device use fell to 55% by 6 months in one study ([Woodham 2025](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:cd3d8bca-5bdc-4efe-864e-ad76b829e08d/)). This is why the stack uses one short daily check-in, not five.

There is an attractive research idea behind denser check-ins. In self-recorded emotion data, upcoming shifts into or out of depression were preceded by **rising autocorrelation, variance and correlation between emotions** — the "critical slowing down" seen near tipping points in other complex systems ([van de Leemput et al. 2014, PNAS](https://research.tilburguniversity.edu/en/publications/critical-slowing-down-as-early-warning-for-the-onset-and-terminat/)) [verified abstract]. This was shown at group level. A single-patient study (Wichers et al. 2016) reported the warning signal about a month before relapse, but later multi-person studies found only modest person-level sensitivity [all UNVERIFIED]. The signal needs several prompts a day and weeks of baseline. For the product, a rolling "instability index" (variance and lag-1 autocorrelation of daily mood) is a reasonable **exploratory** feature for the learning layer, but not an alarm.

### Item 9 needs a real-time human pathway

Item 9 asks about "thoughts that you would be better off dead or of hurting yourself." In 84,418 outpatients who completed 207,265 PHQ-9s, one-year suicide attempts were **0.4% among those answering "not at all" vs 4% among those answering "nearly every day"**, and suicide deaths **0.03% vs 0.3%**. **Excess risk appeared within several days and kept growing for several months**, which suggests a lasting vulnerability rather than a short crisis ([Simon et al. 2013](https://psychiatryonline.org/doi/abs/10.1176/appi.ps.201200587)) [verified]. The risk for the middle answers ("several days", "more than half the days") was not shown on the page read. THL's Finnish slides give the item wording but **no handling protocol** ([THL/Innokylä](https://innokyla.fi/sites/default/files/2023-06/Linnaranta_PHQ-9%20saavutettava.pdf)) [verified]. The usual practice — a structured follow-up such as the C-SSRS screener, then a safety plan (Stanley-Brown), with same-day clinician review in collaborative-care programmes — is [UNVERIFIED]. Post-TMS patients have treatment-resistant depression, so positive item-9 answers will occur. **This pathway is a core feature, not an edge case**, and it must be agreed with the clinical partner before any real user sees the question.

Three patterns stand out. First, **the behavioural signals (steps, rest-activity rhythm) have the largest samples and the lowest burden**, and they map directly onto DSM symptoms (fatigue, psychomotor slowing, sleep). They are the right first features for a personal baseline. Second, **HRV is real but must be read within one person**: medication shifts it more than depression does, and the Oura ring under-reads RMSSD in a systematic way, so trends from one device are more trustworthy than comparisons with published values. Third, **voice and EEG are the most "deep-tech" signals and the least proven.** Voice has the stronger within-person evidence of the two (Mundt, Lin, Wang), provided the recording prompt is fixed. EEG has portable hardware that reproduces lab spectra, but its only home within-person example is a 10-person pilot, and the reviews state plainly that no validated method exists. For the challenge host's portable-EEG interest, the defensible framing is: **"a within-person trend of resting spectral features, anchored to the clinic's pre-TMS recording, as a research layer"** — not "detects depression from EEG". Facial-expression features were not researched in depth; E-DAIC provides them pre-extracted ([E-DAIC manual](https://dcapswoz.ict.usc.edu/wwwedaic/E-DAIC%20Manual.pdf)). Phone-based cognitive tests (reaction time, working memory) were **not researched** and remain a gap.

## Six design rules follow from the evidence gaps

The evidence leads to a small set of design rules. Each is an inference for the team to test, not a finding.

**Learn direction, not just size.** Atypical and melancholic patterns move sleep and appetite in opposite directions, and prodromes are consistent within a person. The model should store, per user, which signals moved in which direction before their last episode, starting from the discharge interview. A two-sided deviation score (too much or too little sleep) is safer than a one-sided rule.

**Wait for a baseline before alerting.** Group-level effects (HRV g ≈ −0.46, step RR 0.91 per 1,000 steps) are too small and too confounded by age and medication to set absolute thresholds. Collect 2–4 weeks of passive and check-in data after discharge before any sensor-driven prompt fires. Medication changes should reset or annotate the HRV baseline.

**Let sensors ask, let scales decide, let humans confirm.** Sensor deviation triggers an extra PHQ-9; the PHQ-9 triggers the clinician list; the clinician interview confirms relapse. This three-step chain respects the known false-positive rate (about half of PHQ-9 positives in primary care) and the internally validated nature of sensor models. It also gives the clinician view a short, explainable reason for each name on the "who might need a call" list.

**Treat missing data as information, but carefully.** The GPS study excluded 12 of 40 people for insufficient data, and those may have been the most withdrawn. A drop in wear time or check-in completion is worth a nudge and, if it persists, a "lost contact" tag. It should not count as evidence of relapse on its own.

**Fix the voice prompt and the language.** If the team includes voice, record the same short reading passage each time, in the user's own language, and model only change from that person's baseline. No Finnish or Swedish voice-depression studies were found, so population classifiers trained on English data should not be used.

**Keep EEG out of the alarm path.** Use portable EEG as a monthly research measurement linked to the clinic's pre-TMS recording, which also suits the Aalto TMS group's research interests. It can feed the learning layer later, once within-person evidence exists.

## Conclusion

The science of depression gives the product a clear target — nine DSM symptoms plus hopelessness and anxiety — and clear time rules (3 weeks for remission, 4 months for recovery) that let the clinician view say whether a person is in non-remission, relapse or recurrence. What it does not yet give is a validated *sensor* for relapse. The most useful reframing for the pitch is therefore that the innovation is not a new biomarker but **a closed loop that uses cheap passive signals to time a validated question, and uses the answer to time a human call**. That design is defensible today, uses the same scale HUS already uses at referral, and can absorb better signals (voice, EEG) as their within-person evidence matures.

The biggest open questions are the ones the team could turn into a research story with the Aalto TMS group: whether a personal relapse signature recorded at TMS discharge predicts the next episode, whether once-daily check-ins stay above useful adherence for 6 months, and whether a deviation-triggered PHQ-9 catches worsening earlier than a fixed schedule. None of these has been tested after TMS. Before any real user sees the PHQ-9, the item-9 pathway, the crisis-line number and the regulatory status need confirmation with the clinical partner.
